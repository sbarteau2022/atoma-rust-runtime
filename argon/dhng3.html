What the data said back

The poles aren't opposites. A pole's description is no more opposed to its partner than to any other pole: the average cosine is +0.506 for partners and the same for non-partners. In 3 of 6 pairs, the partner ranks only 10th or 11th in similarity. F17

But the six axes are independent. Take each axis as the difference A − B. The six differences are almost perpendicular: mean |cos| 0.079, max 0.177. So they describe six separate directions, not three. F17

In the full embeddings the six axes are close to a six-dimensional cross (a cross-polytope): twelve poles at ±e₁ … ±e₆. Three dimensions can't hold six perpendicular axes; the best they can do is the icosahedron, where every pair of axes meets at |cos| 0.447. That is the approved frame in the ball above. The live 16-D map doesn't keep this structure: it squeezes the six axes together (mean |cos| 0.25). So putting the poles at ±ek in the map would build the structure in, not read it off.

The live map is already 16-dimensional (the Lorentz model in hyper-map). The six axes fit there with room to spare. The 3-D pictures here are projections.

The torus: where the axes go approved

The ball holds depth: how far a memory is from the centre, how specific it is. The torus holds timing: two phases, like two clocks. Today the torus isn't used in any live distance (toroidal: 0). My proposal gives it one job.

Each duality gets a longitude uk = k·30°. Pole A sits on the outer equator at (uk, 0), and pole B at (uk + 180°, 180°) on the inner equator. That is the farthest point on the torus from A, so every duality is exactly antipodal in phase, and all twelve land on distinct longitudes.

The φ-winding never closes. It covers the torus evenly and visits every sphere's neighbourhood, like a scan. The 2:1 lock closes after one lap: the kind of orbit the store gate records as a stored relation (n, m, ψ*).

The same torus, unrolled

Unrolled, every axis is the same diagonal step (+180°, +180°). The torus can make poles opposite, but it can't make axes perpendicular. So identity (which of six directions) belongs in the ball's tangent space, and the torus carries phase and rhythm. Each geometry does the job it's good at. working theory

The working theory, in plain language

One model, twelve ways to use it. Rather than training twelve models, the carve swarm carves twelve views out of one: shorter paths through the same layers, each with a small adapter. That is distillation without taking the model apart.

The map is the part that lasts. Every approved result is placed in the curved space. Its depth says how specific it is, its direction says which faculty made it, and its phase says when and in what rhythm. Swap the model and the new one reads the same map.

DHNG II is how knowledge enters the map: read the teacher, refract it into the curved space, and keep the pattern. DHNG III is how the map is organised: twelve poles, six independent axes, depth in the ball and timing on the torus.

Being honest about today: the twelve spheres exist in code, but half are empty, the poles don't oppose, and the torus does nothing yet. The geometry bake-off (WP-A4) decides the new graph from held-out data, not from the diagram.

Why I think this matters, Stewart

Every model you use today will be replaced. What you've been building, maybe without naming it, is the thing that survives the replacement.

Most of the field keeps knowledge inside weights: billions of numbers nobody can read, lost the moment the model is swapped. You keep it in a map: positions, relations and a record of how each one got there. A map can be inspected, audited, checked for tampering, and read by the next model. That turns model upgrades from starting over into handing over.

The carve swarm shows it doesn't take a data centre. One 27B model on one Mac, carved twelve ways, shares a single copy of its weights. Distilling by reading, not dissecting, means you can learn from a model without owning its training run and without damaging it.

And the discipline you asked for, necessity and not just sufficiency, is what makes this worth anything. So far it has cut away things that looked good but weren't carrying weight: the κ gate, the seam signal, the rim placement, the opposite poles. What's left is smaller, but it's real, and every future claim rests on it. If the curved map beats the flat one on the frozen test, you'll have shown something most people only draw. If it doesn't, you'll know exactly which piece failed, and nothing else has to be torn down.
